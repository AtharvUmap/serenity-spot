Personal Reflection

Reflection

A space to think out loud about what this project taught me, and what I'm still sitting with.

Many of these reflections came out of an interview with Pamela Scott, my instructor for this course and the person who oversaw my efforts most closely across both semesters. The conversation moved through three threads: sustenance, process, and learning. The entries below trace each in turn, and close with what the semester taught me about my own leadership.

Entry 01

Sustaining the spark.

The first idea that stayed with me is sustenance, but not in the way I expected. When someone asked me, "how have you seen my change effort progress?", I thought I'd talk about longevity, or the foundation I'd built for the future of the project. The answer that came back was different: what they had noticed in me was the sustained enthusiasm for the work itself, across two semesters of slow and uneven progress.

I was told I'd done an excellent job keeping my energy and motivation up even at moments when it would have been easy to disconnect. That landed because, honestly, those moments were real. There were stretches where I wasn't sure what to do next, and a real low point when I learned I wouldn't be able to hand the site off to a design group this semester. What I took from the conversation was that I'd collected myself well enough, and held a positive outward-facing mentality, that the people around me couldn't always see the doubt.

That is the lesson I want to keep. A leader has to hold both things at once: be honestly wary of the obstacles, the timelines, and the risks, while still carrying a visible optimism that others can rally around. You are the face of your own effort, and if your energy dips publicly, the project's energy dips with it. Being able to sustain that level of enthusiasm, and share it with every actor I ended up talking to, is, looking back, one of the biggest positives of this whole experience.

Entry 02

The space between knowing and doing.

Most of the project's friction lived in the gap between planning and doing. The planning phase had been thorough: the research, the conversations, the case I'd built. I knew what needed to happen next. The hard part was the implementation, where institutional progress stalled in a different way: I was continuously referred from person to person, with no streamlined path through the university. Even when I had a clear ask, getting it in front of the right person was its own multi-step project. There were stretches where the bottleneck wasn't the idea or the energy, but simply the act of sitting down and pushing the next call through.

The takeaway from the conversation was to reflect on the process while I'm still in it, not only at the end. If I'd treated the implementation phase as something to study in real time, building a small habit or pattern around how I track contacts and follow up, I could have turned ambiguity into a checklist for everyone involved. The exact path can't be standardized, every project and every university shapes the route differently, but the habit of writing down the route as you walk it is portable. That is what I am carrying into the next project I do.

Entry 03

A ripple of change.

One of the last things that has been sitting with me is the idea of unknown effects, the impact you can't see and won't get to track. A lot of my recent conversations have circled the disappointment of not seeing this project come fully to fruition this semester. On the surface, the delay is real: the seating and the redesign of the pathway haven't happened yet, which makes it easy to read the impact as limited.

A perspective shift came in one of those conversations: it is impossible to track how each meeting, each call, and each piece of writing inspires the people on the other side of it. The scope of impact is almost certainly wider than I can perceive. My work this semester may quietly give someone else permission to believe they can do this too, and that they can act on the issues they care about. That, more than the bench itself, is the world I want to live in, one where everyone believes they can be a leader and make social change.

This isn't an abstract idea for me. The original spark for The Serenity Spot came from watching a friend pour themselves into something cool for their own community. That action was the ripple I felt, and it taught me that passion and action don't argue people into believing they can lead, they simply show them. I'll keep building these ripples through this site, and I thoroughly hope they turn into a wave that betters both the UMD and the global community.

Entry 04

The leader in me.

At the start of my leadership journey, I used to run away from leadership positions. I associated leaders with being confident, loud, and charismatic, what I now know as "The Great Man Theory." Progressing through the leadership minor, my MLEAD internship, and this course has not only shattered that assumption but let me thrive inside the work it once kept me out of.

The shift has been quiet but real: I've built self-efficacy across every domain I've stepped into, in sports, in my technical background, and now in a field I had no prior experience in whatsoever. I leave this semester assured of my own leadership across very different dimensions, and confident in whatever comes next.

Meaning-Making

What this project means to me, now.

This project is a symbol for what I am capable of achieving, and what any individual can do when they put their mind and heart into it. I could have chosen a Computer Science-related project. I could have decided to take on something less time-intensive to secure a guaranteed result. But I didn't. I chose passion, I chose change, I chose my community. This project will serve as a reminder of what leadership means to me, and the values I hope to represent throughout my personal and professional life.

"Know yourself. Know what you stand for. All that's left is to show you to the world."
Atharv Umap, May 2026
