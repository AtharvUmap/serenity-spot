Get Involved

The Serenity Spot is just one student's project, but the playbook isn't mine to keep. If you've ever noticed a gap on your own campus and wondered how to start, this is for you.

Starter Kit

Start your own greenspace
or mental-health project.

Eight steps, in the order we wish we'd done them. Adapt freely; the framework is more useful than the specifics.

01

Find the gap that bothers you.

+
Walk your campus the way a stranger would. Where do students avoid? Where do they linger? The gap that catches your eye is the one you'll have energy to keep returning to. Don't pick a problem because it's "important", pick the one that nags at you.
02

Talk to ten people before you write anything.

+

Five peers, three administrators, two staff members. Not to pitch, to listen. You'll find out fast whether the gap is real, who's already tried to address it, and where the political landmines are.

I did this twice for The Serenity Spot. The first pass was a Campus Connections exercise: I mapped ten people inside UMD whose work touched the project, the Vice President for Mental Health and Wellbeing, a senior campus planner, Facilities staff, peer student leaders, and faculty advisors. The second pass was a Social Change Connections exercise: ten people outside the university whose perspective on greenspace, mental health, or community design I needed before I committed to a direction. Doing both meant I walked into the proposal phase already knowing what objections would come and who I'd want in the room when they did.

03

Navigating calls and discussions.

+
Before you reach out to anyone on your list, write down what you want to learn from that specific person, in one sentence. Send the meeting request with that purpose stated up front. People give better time when they know exactly why you asked, and you'll leave each call with something concrete instead of a polite chat.
04

Find the unlikely ally.

+
Look for someone whose job is already pointed at your problem but who has the institutional knowledge you don't. For me, it was UMD's Vice President for Mental Health and Wellbeing, who didn't just validate the project but pointed me toward the Student Facilities Fund as a real funding pathway, something I wouldn't have known to ask about on my own. The right ally doesn't only open doors; they tell you which doors are worth knocking on.
05

Set both a small goal and a large one.

+
Pair an ambition you can deliver yourself with one that needs the right people in the room. For The Serenity Spot, the small goal was simple: add more seating to the pathway. The large goal was to hand the site off to a design group who could layer in the therapeutic and design elements I couldn't build alone. The small goal proves the gap is real; the large goal is what makes the project worth building.
06

Document everything, even if no one else sees it.

+
Photos, quotes, head counts, before-and-afters, who said what in which meeting. The documentation doesn't have to live publicly to be useful: a clear, well-structured record is how you walk into the next conversation knowing exactly what's already been said and what's still open. If you do choose to make some of it public (this site is mine), it becomes the easiest way to make the project real to people who weren't there.
07

Sustain the project after you.

+
You will graduate. The work has to be able to outlive your involvement, which means writing down the things you'll forget and leaving them somewhere others can find. This website, along with the process behind it, is my way of sustaining The Serenity Spot and its effort, a public record of the case, the contacts, and the path forward, so the project doesn't reset every time a new student picks it up.
08

Take care of yourself, too.

+
You're trying to build a place where other students can rest. Don't drive yourself into the ground doing it. The work has to be liveable for you, or it teaches the wrong lesson.
